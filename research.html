Research

Traffic engineering has long described behavior with parameters — a desired speed, a headway, a gap-acceptance threshold. Real drivers and real travelers do not think in parameters. My work asks whether language models, which are built to read context, can carry that behavioral context into the models we already use to plan and evaluate transportation systems.

Driving behavior with LLMs

Trajectory data tells you what a vehicle did. It does not tell you why. Two drivers with the same speed profile may be doing entirely different things — one is impatient in free flow, the other is trapped behind a slow truck.

I cluster real-world trajectories into behavioral types, describe each type in natural language, and use an LLM to turn those descriptions back into driving decisions. The generated agents are then dropped into SUMO and VISSIM, where the question becomes empirical: does the synthetic population reproduce the distribution of the observed one?

This was the subject of my master's thesis, and it is the line of work I most want to push further — toward driving behavior that responds to context rather than being sampled from a fixed distribution.

Related: TRB 2026 (first author) · KSITS 2025 · KST 2025 (Best Paper) · ITS Asia-Pacific Forum 2025 (Best Paper)

Human mobility synthesis

Individual-level mobility data is the most useful and the most sensitive dataset in transportation. Travel surveys are rich but small; mobile location data is large but blunt, and neither can be released as-is.

At the Korea Transport Institute I worked on generating synthetic individuals — full daily travel chains with plausible activity sequences, timing, and destinations — that hold at the aggregate level without exposing any real person. Retrieval-augmented LLMs let the generator condition on survey evidence rather than inventing from scratch, which is what makes the output defensible.

The Sejong City case study was the first full application, and the method is being extended to nationwide travel data.

Related: Journal of Korean Society of Transportation 45(2) · KST Fall 2025 · KSITS 2026

Traffic safety & risk

Crash records are the ground truth for safety, but they are rare, lagging, and silent about the near-misses that precede them. Microscopic simulation produces conflicts in abundance but has no claim to realism on its own.

I build hybrid frameworks that use each to correct the other: crash data anchors the model where observations exist, and simulation extends risk estimates into conditions that have not yet been observed. Related work extends this to autonomous-vehicle legal compliance, LiDAR-based traffic measurement, and child-pedestrian trajectory prediction.
